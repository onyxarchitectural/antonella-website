Montessori at Home: 7 Simple Activities to Build Independence in Toddlers

Montessori MethodBy Antonella RoasJune 12, 2025
⏱ 6 min read

As a child psychologist working with families across Coral Gables and greater Miami, one of the most common questions I hear from parents is: "How can I bring Montessori principles into our home without a full classroom setup?" The good news is that Montessori is less about expensive materials and more about a mindset — one that trusts children to do things for themselves.

Toddlers between 12 and 36 months are in a critical window for developing autonomy, fine motor skills, and self-confidence. In this article, I'll share practical, low-cost Montessori-inspired activities you can start using this week, along with insights from child development research on why these activities matter so much for your little one's growing brain.

Why Independence Matters So Early

Between ages one and three, children enter what Maria Montessori called the "sensitive period for order and independence." During this stage, toddlers have a biological drive to do things themselves — pouring water, putting on shoes, cleaning up toys. When we constantly do these tasks for them, we unintentionally send the message that they are not capable. Over time, this can affect self-esteem and increase frustration-driven tantrums.

Allowing age-appropriate independence, even when it's slower or messier, builds neural pathways associated with executive function: planning, sequencing, and impulse control. These are the same skills your child will rely on for handwriting, following multi-step instructions in preschool, and managing emotions.

💡 Antonellas Tip: Resist the urge to correct or redo a task your toddler just attempted. Praise the effort ("You worked so hard putting on your shoes!") rather than the perfection of the result.

Setting Up a Montessori-Friendly Space at Miami Homes

You don't need a dedicated playroom to apply Montessori principles — even a small corner of a Coral Gables condo or a Miami apartment can work. The key is accessibility: everything your toddler needs should be within their reach and sized for their small hands and bodies.

Consider a low shelf with 3-4 rotating activity trays, a step stool in the bathroom so they can wash their own hands, and a low hook for their jacket by the door. In our humid South Florida climate, a small basket with sunscreen, a sun hat, and water shoes near the entrance also invites toddlers to help prepare for outdoor time — a wonderful daily independence ritual.

7 Montessori Activities for Toddlers

Here are activities I recommend to the families I work with, organized from easiest to more advanced:

1. Pouring practice: Use two small pitchers and let your child pour water (or dry rice indoors) from one to another.

2. Dressing frames: Practice buttons, zippers, and snaps using an old shirt or a specific dressing board.

3. Food preparation: Let toddlers wash fruit, spread butter with a dull knife, or peel a banana.

4. Sorting activities: Sort seashells or buttons by color and size — a great weekend activity after a trip to a Miami beach.

5. Practical life care: Watering a small plant, sweeping with a child-sized broom, or wiping a table after snack time.

6. Sensory bins: Fill a bin with sand, dried beans, or water beads for open-ended tactile exploration.

7. Self-dressing station: A low basket with 2-3 outfit choices lets toddlers make decisions and dress independently.

Common Challenges Parents Face

Many Miami parents tell me they worry about mess, time constraints during busy mornings, or grandparents who prefer to "just do it faster." These are valid concerns, especially in bilingual, multigenerational households common in our community.

My suggestion: start with just one activity, ideally on a weekend or slower morning, and gradually build it into the routine. Communicate with caregivers about the "why" behind Montessori independence — framing it as skill-building rather than extra work often helps everyone get on board.

When to Seek Professional Guidance

While most toddlers thrive with these simple adjustments, some children show ongoing frustration, resistance to any independence tasks, or delays in fine motor coordination that go beyond typical variation. If you notice your child consistently avoiding self-help tasks, showing extreme meltdowns during transitions, or falling behind developmental milestones, a professional evaluation can offer clarity and a personalized plan.

Quick Tips for Getting Started This Week

  • Choose just one Montessori activity to introduce first, rather than overhauling your whole routine at once.
  • Lower a shelf or basket so your child can access their own toys, snacks, or clothing.
  • Allow extra time in the morning routine for your toddler to attempt dressing or hand washing independently.
  • Narrate what you see them doing instead of jumping in to fix mistakes.
  • Rotate materials every 1-2 weeks to keep novelty and engagement high.

Conclusion

Bringing Montessori principles into your Miami home doesn't require a complete renovation or a big budget — it starts with small shifts in how much space, time, and trust we give our toddlers to do things themselves. These early experiences with independence lay the foundation for confidence, problem-solving, and emotional regulation that will serve your child well beyond the toddler years.
